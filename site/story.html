Iron Mike

One object, sixty years of paperwork, and the people who worked around it.

Placeholder text

The Iron Mike story already exists and is the reason this project got started. The text below is sample copy standing in for it — it shows how a feature reads on top of the catalog, with records cited inline, without putting words in anyone’s mouth. The real story would be dropped in here as-is.

Every unit has one. The thing that was always there, that nobody can quite date, that three different people will tell you three different stories about. Ask around long enough and you will get a name, a decade, and a shrug.

What an archive is good for is settling that. Not with a better memory, but with paper: a requisition, a photograph with a date on the verso, a line in a station log. Put enough of those next to each other and the shrug turns into a timeline.

What the paperwork says

The earliest reference in the collection is a single index card — chassis specifications, pump ratings, an assignment history running about fifteen years in three different hands. It is not a story. It is the skeleton a story hangs on.

The photographs are harder. There are plenty of them, and almost none are captioned. A print from the late fifties shows a line of men in front of a station that has since been rebuilt twice; the faces are sharp and the names are gone.

You can preserve an object and still lose everything that made it matter. The object is the easy part. — sample pull quote, standing in for the real interview

Where the record runs out

This is the point in every one of these stories where the documents stop and the people start. Somebody’s father took the photograph. Somebody’s uncle is third from the left. That information is not in the archive room and never was — it is in garages, photo albums, and the heads of people who retired twenty years ago.

Which is the argument for putting any of this online in the first place. A closed archive can only ever answer questions. An open one collects them.

Can you help?

There are faces, apparatus, and locations in this collection that nobody has been able to name. If you worked here, or your family did, you probably know something no finding aid will ever capture.
